Local and API models as stand-ins for Jev

This report tests whether a model you run yourself, or a cheap API model, can stand in for Jev by matching its answers and its confidence, and how fast it answers. It compares 7 systems, Jev included, on 9 benchmarks.

Every number is recomputed from the run records by scripts/make_summary_report.py. Hover over any point for details.

TL;DR (holdout split)

  • Jev is still the most accurate system. On the 6 benchmarks every system answers (7,534 holdout questions), Jev answers 81.6% correctly. The best challenger, Qwen3.6-27B (local), answers 76.8%, a difference of −4.8 points (paired 95% CI −6.7 to −2.9).
  • Much of Jev's lead comes from GPQA-Diamond. Its 99 questions weigh as much in the macro as the 3,800 of AG News, and they account for 49% to 82% of Jev's lead over each challenger. Without GPQA-Diamond, Jev leads by +1.3 to +5.1 points; with every question weighed the same, by +1.2 to +4.5 points. Not all of these paired CIs are above 0.
  • Qwen3.6-27B (local) is statistically tied with DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (API) and GPT-6 Luna (API): the 95% CI of each paired difference includes 0.
  • On one GPU and over all 9 benchmarks, the fastest local finalist, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B GPTQ (local), answers 25.1 questions per second, 5.7× as many as the slowest, Qwen3.6-27B (local) (4.44 q/s). Their accuracy differs by −3.9 points (95% CI −5.2 to −2.7). The other local finalists are in between: Gemma 4 26B-A4B (local) (20.4 q/s), Qwen3.6-35B-A3B AWQ (local) (19.7 q/s).
  • Before calibration, macro ECE ranges from 0.072 to 0.168. One temperature per answer format, fitted without seeing the questions it scores (how), brings every system's macro ECE to between 0.044 and 0.053. Calibrated Brier score, best first: Jev 0.265, Qwen3.6-27B (local) 0.314, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (API) 0.326, GPT-6 Luna (API) 0.340, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B AWQ (local) 0.351, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B GPTQ (local) 0.355, Gemma 4 26B-A4B (local) 0.369.
  • Cost per 1,000 questions on the shared benchmarks, cheapest first: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B GPTQ (local) $0.0006, Gemma 4 26B-A4B (local) $0.0006, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B AWQ (local) $0.0011, Qwen3.6-27B (local) $0.0031, Jev $0.0158, GPT-6 Luna (API) $0.0201, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (API) $0.0439 (local models count electricity only).

Accuracy is the share of questions where the option with the highest probability is correct. A macro average weighs each benchmark the same. q/s is questions answered per second, pooled over every question of a view, so unlike macro accuracy it depends on the benchmark mix. CI is a 95% confidence interval from a bootstrap; paired means two systems compared on the same questions. Points are percentage points. The Brier score is the squared error between the option probabilities and the correct answer, from 0 (perfect) to 2; lower is better. ECE (expected calibration error) is the average gap between a system's confidence in its top answer and how often that answer is right; 0 is perfect. Raw probabilities are as returned; calibrated ones are after temperature scaling (how).

Systems compared

Jev (jev-1.13.0) answers multiple-choice and classification questions with a probability for every option. The emulator in this repository asks an open model the same question. It reads the probabilities from the model's next-token distribution over the option labels, in one model call per question. Questions with more than 32 options (banking77, CLINC150) get two-capital labels (AA, AB, …), each one token, instead of letters. Local models use vLLM 0.30.0 on one RTX 3090 (24 GB). API models run with reasoning turned off and must reply {"answer": "<label>"} with a label from a fixed list (Structured Outputs); the probabilities come from the logprobs (log probabilities) where the label starts. CLM-8B (Contrastive-LM/CLM), shown on the screen split only, is a dual encoder: Qwen3-8B embeds the state and question once and each option's text on its own, and a 20M-parameter head scores the options by scaled cosine similarity. It answers Jev's wire format, one request per question, on one RTX 3090. In the figures, filled markers are local models (CLM-8B included) and hollow markers are API services.

Speed limits

Local runs kept 16 questions in flight. API runs also went through our client's request limiter, so their q/s (marked †) is the limiter's rate. The dotted arrows in the speed figures end at the q/s each service's own limits would allow without the limiter. The ceilings below are computed for the 6 benchmarks every system answers; none of them was measured.

  • Jev ran at 20.0 q/s with 16 in flight, with our limiter set to 1,200 requests/min (20.0 q/s). The ceiling without the limiter is ≈605 q/s, bound by 250,000 tokens/s at 413 tokens per question. Over all 9 benchmarks it is ≈273 q/s, bound by 250,000 tokens/s at 917 tokens per question. Jev documents limits of 1,200 requests/min and 250,000 tokens/s. These runs sent one question per request, so 1,200 requests/min is exactly 20 q/s. One request can carry many questions, though, so the token limit is the actual bound, assuming it counts the tokens Jev reports, internal ones included. This bound is inferred from the docs and was not measured.
  • GPT-6 Luna (API) ran at 7.47 q/s with 16 in flight, with our limiter set to 450 requests/min (7.50 q/s). The ceiling without the limiter is ≈8.33 q/s, bound by 500 requests/min, one question per request (other bounds: 200,000 tokens/min at 157 tokens per question: 21.2; 16 requests in flight at 684 ms mean latency: 23.4). The OpenAI account used here has limits of 500 requests and 200,000 tokens per minute (read from response headers); higher usage tiers allow more. Beyond the account limits, q/s is bounded by the requests in flight divided by the measured latency.
  • DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (API) ran at 7.38 q/s with 16 in flight, with our limiter set to 450 requests/min (7.50 q/s). The ceiling without the limiter is ≈40.6 q/s, bound by 16 requests in flight at 394 ms mean latency. OpenRouter sets no request limit on paid models, and the Makora provider's own limit was not measured. These runs kept 16 requests in flight. The bound shown is therefore our own in-flight setting (16 here) divided by the measured latency; it grows with that setting and is not a published service limit.

Every local number comes from one machine, vLLM 0.30.0 on one RTX 3090 (24 GB), with presets tuned for that card, 16 questions in flight and no client limiter. So q/s is the number in flight divided by the mean latency (on the 6 benchmarks every system answers: Qwen3.6-27B (local) 9.98 q/s measured, 16 ÷ 1,593 ms = 10.0 q/s; Qwen3.6-35B-A3B AWQ (local) 28.1 q/s measured, 16 ÷ 567 ms = 28.2 q/s; Qwen3.6-35B-A3B GPTQ (local) 53.2 q/s measured, 16 ÷ 299 ms = 53.5 q/s; Gemma 4 26B-A4B (local) 52.3 q/s measured, 16 ÷ 304 ms = 52.6 q/s).

Another GPU, engine (llama.cpp, MLX, SGLang), quantization or concurrency level can change q/s several-fold, up or down. The fastest small models reached the limit of 16 in flight before the GPU's. Local q/s ranks the models on this card; your machine will give other numbers. Running the same benchmark on a Mac (llama.cpp or MLX) is on the to-do list (docs/TODO.md) and has not been done.

Cost: token prices for APIs; for local models, the GPU's electricity: 410 W at $0.27/kWh = $0.1107 per GPU-hour (hardware not included).

Calibration and reliability on holdout

A reliability diagram groups questions by the probability a system gave its top answer. It then plots how often that answer was right. A calibrated system sits on the diagonal; points below the diagonal mean overconfidence.

Bins have equal width, so the points cover every confidence level that occurs. Marker area shows how many questions a bin holds; bins with fewer than 10 are left out. The top answer's probability can never be below 1 divided by the number of options: 50% on BoolQ, 25% on four-option questions.

How calibration works

Calibration changes a system's confidence and leaves the answer it picks unchanged.

The method is temperature scaling. A question has K options, with probabilities p1…pK. Each is replaced by p′i ∝ pi1/T and the results are renormalized to sum to 1. T is one fitted number: T > 1 softens overconfident answers (a 95% may become 85%), T < 1 sharpens underconfident ones. The order of the options never changes, so the top answer and the accuracy stay exactly the same. Only the confidence moves, and with it the Brier score, the NLL (negative log-likelihood, also called log loss) and the ECE. T is chosen to minimize the log loss on the fitting questions.

The calibrator, temperature@signature, fits one temperature per answer format. A question's signature is its type plus its exact option labels. All A to D multiple-choice questions share one signature; BoolQ's two options have another; banking77's 77 intents another. A model can be overconfident on one format and underconfident on another, so each format gets its own T. A format with fewer than 30 fitting questions uses one global T instead. Each system gets its own temperatures, fitted on its own answers only.

The temperatures are fitted out of fold. A system's questions are split into 5 folds. The folds are the same for every system, and the items of one question stay together. Each fold is calibrated with temperatures fitted on the other 4, so no question is scored by a fit that saw it. The fit still comes from the same benchmarks and split, though. A deployed calibrator is fitted once, on a separate split.

Formulas

The model is asked once, and ℓi is the log-probability of option i's label token at the answer position (the forms A and  A of one label are added together). The K labels' probabilities are renormalized so they sum to 1:

pi=eℓi∑j=1Keℓj

A label missing from the returned top-k gets an upper bound instead of zero: the smallest returned log-probability or, in the API models' structured read (where only labels can follow), the probability the listed labels leave over. Jev returns its probabilities directly.

Temperature scaling, written in two equivalent forms:

pi′=pi1/T∑j=1Kpj1/T=eℓi/T∑j=1Keℓj/T

For each signature s, the fitted temperature Ts minimizes the mean log loss over its Ns fitting questions, where gq is question q's correct option. T is searched between 0.001 and 1000.

Ts=argminT1Ns∑q∈s−logpgq′(T)

The metrics per question use pg, the probability of the correct option, and yi, which is 1 for the correct option and 0 for the others. NLL is clipped at 10−6 so a probability of exactly 0 stays finite; Brier runs from 0 (perfect) to 2.

NLL=−logmax(pg,10−6) Brier=∑i=1K(pi−yi)2

ECE is computed per benchmark. Each question's confidence is its top option's probability, maxi pi. Questions are sorted by confidence and split into 10 groups of equal size; for group b, accb is the share answered correctly and confb the mean confidence. The reliability figures use fixed-width bins instead, for display only.

ECE=∑b=110nbN|accb−confb|

Accuracy, NLL, Brier and ECE are computed per benchmark, then averaged over the benchmarks with equal weight (macro).

For example, GPT-6 Luna (API) has the highest raw ECE. On the 6 benchmarks every system answers, calibration takes its ECE from 0.168 to 0.053 and its Brier score from 0.396 to 0.340, while its accuracy stays at 75.6%.

Caveats

  • Speed is not measured like for like. Local models share one consumer GPU and one engine (vLLM), and your hardware will give different numbers. API numbers are bounded by our client's request limiter and by network latency. Jev can answer several questions per request, which these runs did not use.
  • Calibration is fitted on the evaluation half. The fit is out of fold (5 folds), so no item is scored by a fit that saw it, but it comes from the same distribution. A deployed calibrator is fitted once, on a whole split.
  • Accuracy uses the argmax of the probabilities, the option with the highest probability. An "I don't know" answer counts as wrong. On a handful of near-ties, Jev's returned choice differs from its argmax.
  • vLLM is not deterministic on this setup, so runs do not repeat exactly. Sending byte-identical requests again flipped the top answer on 4 to 7% of hard multiple-choice questions (GPQA-Diamond, LEXam, MMLU-Pro; about 0.3% on ARC). The GPTQ model's GPQA-Diamond accuracy ranged from 54.5% to 57.6% across runs. In a probe, 2 of 100 of GPT-6 Luna's top answers also flipped between identical calls. The 95% intervals cover only the sampling of questions and leave this noise out. The emulators and API models (73.0% to 76.8% on the 6 benchmarks every system answers) therefore cannot be ranked when they are within about 1 point of each other.
  • GPQA-Diamond's holdout half is easier than its select half: every system scores 8 to 18 points higher on it (permutation test p = 0.014). The split is a random hash, the same for every system, so this is luck of the draw. This is why every system scores about 2 points higher on holdout than on select over the shared benchmarks, and why Gemma's select-to-holdout jump is bigger.
  • API models answer in JSON. Local models are made to start their reply with “Answer:”, so the next token is the label, but the hosted APIs do not continue such a prefill. API models therefore must reply {"answer": "<label>"} with a label from a fixed list (Structured Outputs), and the probabilities are read where the label starts, where only labels can follow. On hard or borderline questions this wrapping can change the model's pick compared with a free reply.
  • GPT-6 Luna (API) lists only a few labels: OpenAI returns at most 5 alternatives and drops unlikely ones, so on 98% of its holdout questions some option is missing. A missing option gets the probability the listed labels leave over, at most 2.0%. That is an upper bound, since only labels can follow there, and its raw NLL depends on it. Its calibrated numbers barely do (NLL moves by at most 0.02 even with the missing options at 0), and its accuracy not at all (a missing option is never the top answer).
  • DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (API) left some questions unanswered: 3 of 1,105 SST-5 questions on holdout, 3 on select. Its reply never reached the answer, even when sent again. It is scored on the questions it answered, and paired differences use the questions both systems answered.
  • Jev rounds its probabilities to 0.01. On 409 of its 17,340 holdout questions the correct option gets exactly 0, which NLL clips to 10−6 (13.8 nats each). Jev's raw NLL (in metrics.json) is therefore inflated, and raw-NLL gaps understate its lead. This page compares Brier and ECE; calibrated NLL is barely affected.
  • Local cost covers electricity only: the RTX 3090 draws about 410 W while running, priced at the New Jersey average. It excludes the GPU and the machine.

Method and reproduction

Splits are fixed per benchmark: screen ⊂ select, and both are disjoint from holdout. Macro numbers weigh every benchmark equally. Their 95% intervals come from a stratified bootstrap (10,000 resamples). ECE uses 10 equal-count bins per benchmark; the reliability diagrams use equal-width bins.

Every number on this page is in the metrics.json file next to it. More detail is in docs/research/selection.md, docs/research/calibration.md, reports/speed_quality and reports/jev_vs_qwen.
